# 1-3-1 Zone / Trap Defense

**Family:** disrupting zone — one at the top, three across the FT line, one at the rim.

## Why teams use it
- **Disruption** — trap the corners and wings to force turnovers
- **Surprise weapon** — used in spurts to change tempo
- **Length-friendly** — works well with long-armed defenders

## Strengths
- High turnover potential
- Disrupts rhythm on first possession after timeouts
- Hard to prepare for if not seen often

## Weaknesses (how to attack 1-3-1)
- **Ball reversal** — the wing defender can only cover one side; quick swing exposes the gap.
- **Short corner** — the back defender can't cover both short corners.
- **High post** — flash to the FT line, attack from the middle.
- **Skip passes over the top** — the middle defender is committed; skip clears him.
- **Offensive rebounding** — the back defender is alone vs 2 bigs.

## Scout signals (they're in 1-3-1 if…)
- One defender at top, three across FT-line area, one in the lane
- Frequent traps in the corners and wings
- Disrupted ball reversal (defender chases the swing)

## Famous practitioners
- **John Beilein** (Richmond / West Virginia / Michigan) — the canonical modern 1-3-1 coach; ran it as a primary defense at WVU, less so late at Michigan
- Tubby Smith (Kentucky) — used 1-3-1 trap as a change-up, not a base defense
- Common U Sports change-up; rarely a primary base in the league
