# Full-Court Press Defenses

**Family:** disrupting full-court pressure — used in spurts to force turnovers, change tempo, and tire the offense.

## Common presses

### 1-2-1-1 ("Diamond" press)
- 1 on the inbounder, 2 just past half (trapping), 1 in middle, 1 deep
- **Goal:** force the ball into the middle for a trap
- **Strengths:** great for scrambling unprepared teams
- **Weakness:** great inbounders break it with one pass

### 2-2-1
- 2 across half-court, 2 in the back, 1 deep
- **Goal:** delay tempo, contest cross-court passes
- **Strengths:** safe — rarely gives up easy buckets
- **Weakness:** doesn't generate many turnovers

### 1-3-1 trap full court
- Aggressive — extension of half-court 1-3-1
- **Goal:** double the ball at every catch in the back court

### Run-and-jump
- Defender on ball runs hard at receiver's defender, who jumps the passing lane
- **Goal:** confusion turnovers

## How to attack a press
1. **Get the ball in fast.** Don't let them set up.
2. **Have a designated receiver** with sure hands.
3. **Sprint to spots** — sideline, middle, half-court, basket.
4. **Pass to the middle.** Middle of the press is always open if you can find it.
5. **Throw long if they cheat up.** Layup at the other end punishes overplay.

## When to call it
- Down 8+ late in the game (with 4+ minutes left)
- After timeouts to disrupt rhythm
- Vs poor ball-handling guards

## Scout requirements
For every opponent: do they press? Which press? Do they press after made FTs only or all the time?

## Sources & further reading

### Verified citations (this file's content can be checked against)
- [Full-court press — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full-court_press) — covers history (John McLendon as innovator, Wooden's 1964 UCLA title, Pitino's Kentucky), how presses generate turnovers and tire opponents, and standard press-break principles (short passes beat dribbling; pass to the middle/center).
- [Rick Pitino — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rick_Pitino) — confirms Pitino's Kentucky (1989–1997, 1996 title) and Louisville (2001–2017) tenures, both built on full-court pressure as a season-long identity, not a desperation tactic.
- [Nolan Richardson — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nolan_Richardson) — confirms Arkansas tenure (1985–2002), 1994 NCAA title, and the "40 Minutes of Hell" pressing identity that defined those teams.
- *Full-Court Pressure: A Year in Kentucky Basketball* — Rick Pitino with Dick Weiss (1992) — Pitino's own account of his pressing system and its philosophical underpinnings.
