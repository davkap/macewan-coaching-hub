# Match-Up Zone Defense

**Family:** hybrid zone / man — defenders start in zone positions but **match up to a man** as they enter their area.

## Core principle
**Initial alignment looks like a 2-3 or 1-3-1 zone. But once the ball is entered and players move, defenders pick up whoever enters their zone and play man within their area.**

## Strengths
- Disguises the defense — offense thinks it's zone, attacks gaps that aren't there
- Combines zone protection (paint coverage) with man pressure (denial)
- Hard to scout because it shifts shape based on offensive movement

## Weaknesses (how to attack match-up zone)
- **Quick ball reversal** before defenders can match up
- **Cutter overload** — send 2 cutters into the same zone area, force confusion
- **High-post flash** + skip pass — same zone-attack principles work
- **Off-ball screens** — defenders matching up don't always switch correctly

## Scout signals (you're seeing match-up if…)
- Initial alignment is zone (2-3 or 1-3-1)
- But after the catch, defenders are tight on a man
- Confusion when cutters cross zone boundaries
- They look like zone but cover shooters like man

## Famous practitioners
- **John Chaney** (Temple, 1982–2006) — the godfather of the modern match-up zone; Temple's Owls were the canonical match-up zone team of the 1980s–90s
- **Jim Boeheim** (Syracuse) — most famous 2-3 zone, with match-up principles inside it
- Bob Huggins (West Virginia) — match-up / scramble defense
- Jamie Dixon (Pittsburgh / TCU) — match-up principles in his half-court D
- John Beilein (Michigan/WVU) — variation, more known for the 1-3-1
- Common U Sports and small-college change-up
