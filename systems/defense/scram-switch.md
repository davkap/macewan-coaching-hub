# Scram Switch

**One-line:** A defensive call where a help defender **re-switches** to fix a mismatch created by an earlier switch.

## How it works
1. Ball-screen happens. Defense switches — now the big is on the guard, guard is on the big.
2. The big-on-guard mismatch is bad (drive coming).
3. **Scram:** the next-closest defender (often the weak-side big or wing) leaves his man and switches WITH the mismatched big — eating the original switch.
4. Now the big is on a different perimeter player, and the original guard's defender (who took the big) is restored to a small.

## Why it works
- Eliminates persistent mismatches without having to play them out
- Allows you to switch ball-screens without giving up a long mismatch possession
- The "fix-it" call inside switch-everything

## When to use
- Anytime a switch creates a glaring mismatch you can't live with
- Especially vs teams that hunt mismatches off ball-screens

## Defense rules
- Scram must be **early** — within 2 seconds of the switch
- The scram-help defender must communicate ("SCRAM!")
- The new mismatch (small on a non-shooter) must be acceptable

## How offenses attack scram
- **Throw to the original mismatch immediately** before scram can happen
- **Re-screen** to force another switch
- **Scram into a back-screen** — when the scram defender leaves his man, screen him

### Verification notes (Claude)
- "Scram switch" is genuinely modern NBA terminology (popularized roughly 2017–2020 in public discourse, though the concept of help-side re-switching predates the term). I could not source a single canonical "first use" of the term in this session.
- Erik Spoelstra (Heat) and Brad Stevens / Joe Mazzulla (Celtics) are the most-cited public examples; Steve Kerr's Warriors also use it. The file's framing is consistent with this.
- The "scram into a back-screen" counter is a real, documented offensive answer (Spurs / Heat film) — not fabricated.
- This concept is harder to verify than the others because it lives mostly in NBA staff vocabulary and YouTube film breakdowns, not in books or named clinic systems.
