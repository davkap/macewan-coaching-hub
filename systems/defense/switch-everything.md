# Switch-Everything Defense

**Family:** modern man-to-man. Switch every screen — on-ball, off-ball, all of them.

## Why it's popular now
- Eliminates the chaos of help rotation on ball-screens
- Modern lineups have positional versatility — most NBA & high-major college teams have 5 switchable defenders
- Forces the offense out of its set play immediately

## Principles
1. **Switch every screen.** No exceptions — even big-on-guard.
2. **Late switch = death.** Switches must be early and verbal ("SWITCH!").
3. **Mismatch survival.**
   - Big on guard: drop deep, give up the pull-up, prevent the drive.
   - Guard on big: front the post, expect the lob, weak-side help.
4. **No catch-and-shoot 3s.** Closeouts must be elite.
5. **Scram switches** — when a mismatch occurs, the next available defender re-switches to fix it.

## Strengths
- Kills set-play teams
- Eliminates miscommunication on screens
- Frees up your scheme from complex coverage rules

## Weaknesses (how to attack switch-everything)
- **Mismatch hunting** — set a screen with your worst-matched player. Attack the switch instantly (post-up the small, or iso the big).
- **Slip screens** — screener slips to rim before the switch happens.
- **Ghost screens** — fake screen + pop = uncontested 3 if defenders pre-switch.
- **Spain PnR** — back-screen on the dropping big's defender = unguarded roll.
- **5-out spacing** — no help means switched mismatches play 1-on-1 in space.
- **Re-screens** — force them to switch twice in a possession; communication breaks.

## Scout signals (they're switching everything if…)
- Bigs end up guarding guards on the perimeter
- No drop coverage on ball-screens
- Constant verbal communication on screens
- Fewer rotations / less help

## Famous practitioners
- Brad Stevens / Joe Mazzulla (Celtics)
- Erik Spoelstra (Heat)
- Most modern NBA + most positionless college rosters

## Sources & further reading

### Verified citations (this file's content can be checked against)
- [Pick and roll — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pick_and_roll) — describes "switch" as one of the canonical PnR options and the resulting mismatch problem that switch-everything teams must solve.
- BBALLBREAKDOWN, Thinking Basketball, HalfCourtHoops (YouTube) — extensive film coverage of the 2017–18 Houston Rockets ("switch 1-5") and the 2015–18 Golden State Warriors "Death Lineup" as the modern templates for switch-everything; both teams pioneered switch-1-through-5 with five perimeter-capable defenders.
- Cleaning the Glass — Ben Falk's writing tracks the NBA's swing toward switching as positionless lineups became the norm in the late 2010s.

### Recommended further reading for staff
- Slappin' Glass podcast — multiple episodes with NBA and high-major college assistants on switching rules, scram fixes, and when to switch vs drop.
- FastModel Sports Playbank — search "switch everything" and "switch 1-5" for diagrammed rules and screen coverage.
- *Spaced Out: How the NBA's Three-Point Revolution Changed Everything* — Mike Prada (2022) — has good chapters on the rise of switching as a counter to elite shooting.

### Verification notes (Claude)
- The Rockets (D'Antoni / Mike D'Antoni era, 2016–20) and Warriors (Kerr, "Hampton 5" / Death Lineup) are the canonical NBA switch-everything teams. Their famous personnel — P.J. Tucker, Trevor Ariza, Draymond Green, Andre Iguodala — were specifically built to enable switching 1-through-5.
- Brad Stevens / Joe Mazzulla Celtics and Erik Spoelstra Heat are correctly listed as modern practitioners; both lean heavily on switching with versatile wings (Tatum/Brown/Smart/White; Adebayo as a switchable 5).
- All listed counters (mismatch hunting, slip, ghost, Spain PnR, 5-out spacing, re-screens) are textbook and not in dispute.
- The Mike Prada book is real and recent (2022); coaches should easily find it.
